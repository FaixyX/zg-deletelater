The corridor: motion spec

Every animation on this site answers one question: how does edible oil get from a vessel at Port Qasim to a refinery gate in Punjab, and who guarantees it arrives? Motion that does not serve that story does not ship.

Client — edible oil freight & contract carriage
Design — Osaid
Build — Ali
Review — Faizan

Premise

One corridor, drawn honestly

The organising idea is a single physical route rather than a network diagram. Palm oil discharges at Port Qasim into shore tanks, then moves inland by road tanker along the M-9, N-5, M-5 and M-2 to refineries and ghee mills in Multan, Faisalabad and Lahore.

That corridor is the site's spine. The homepage traces it end to end. Interior pages are points along it. This is the difference between a map that could belong to any freight company and one that could only belong to this client.

Two rules that protect the concept.

Draw only the routes the client actually runs, weighted by volume. Everything else on the map stays faint context. A complete highway network is generic; a weighted one is a credibility claim.

Route animation is viscous, never electric. Liquid filling a pipe, not a data packet firing. Slow, directional, warm. This single choice is what separates the site from every competitor in the category.

Principles

What motion is for here

  1. Weight over speedA tanker carries 30 tonnes. Nothing on this site should feel light, springy or bouncy. No overshoot easings anywhere — back, elastic and bounce are banned outright. Overshoot reads as imprecision, and imprecision is the opposite of what a carriage contract sells.
  2. Motion argues the businessEvery animated element refers to something real: liquid moving, a route being covered, a fleet holding schedule. If an animation cannot be traced to a physical fact about the client's operation, cut it.
  3. One loud momentThe hero map is the memorable thing. Everything below it is quiet, dense and confident. If three sections are animating hard, none of them land.
  4. Scroll is the user's, not oursNo scroll-jacking, no forced pauses, no sections that trap the user until an animation finishes. Scrubbed animations follow the scroll; they never take it over.

Global rules

Applies to every page

RuleValueWhy
Stagger0.08sBelow 0.05s reads as one block; above 0.12s the last item feels late
Max duration1.2sUnless scroll-scrubbed. Users scroll fast and will miss longer sequences
Entrance offset30px, from belowOne direction sitewide. Mixed directions read as unplanned
Trigger pointtop 80%Fires when the element is meaningfully in view, not at the very edge
Replayonce onlyNo re-animating on scroll back. Repeat entrances feel unstable
Hover transition0.25s, SnapHover is feedback, not a performance
Page transition0.4s fadeDeliberately plain. Novelty here costs perceived speed

Non-negotiables

Reduced motion. Every timeline sits behind gsap.matchMedia() with a prefers-reduced-motion: reduce branch that sets final states instantly. This is an accessibility requirement, not a nice-to-have — vestibular disorders make scroll-linked motion genuinely unpleasant.

No layout shift. Animate transform and opacity only. Never animate width, height, top, left or margin — they force layout recalculation on every frame and will drop the site below 60fps on mid-range Android, which is the majority of Pakistani mobile traffic.

Mobile reduction. Below 768px: no scrub-pinned sequences, no parallax, the corridor map becomes a simplified static draw-on-enter. Entrance animations stay. Assume a three-year-old device on a 4G connection.

Homepage

Beat sheet

00Preloader

Dotted outline of Pakistan, centred, in muted steel. A counter runs 00 to 100 in mono, set below the map. As the counter climbs, dots along the corridor illuminate in sequence from Karachi northward — so the load itself traces the route.

At 100 the counter fades, the map scales up and settles into its hero position. It does not disappear and get replaced; it becomes the hero. That continuity is the whole point.

counter Hold tied to real load progress · dot illumination Flow, staggered 0.04s south to north · handoff Carry
hard cap 2.5s — if assets are not ready, proceed anyway and let the hero finish loading behind
01Hero — the live corridor

Map at rest with city nodes marked: Karachi, Hyderabad, Sukkur, Multan, Faisalabad, Lahore. Corridor routes carry a continuous viscous fill moving south to north — oil travelling the pipe. Secondary routes sit faint and static.

Headline and subhead lift in over the top, lines staggered. The map is the background but it is not decorative; it is the product.

route fill Flow, 5s loop, stroke-dashoffset along the path · nodes Flow at 6s offset per node so they never pulse in unison
headline lines Lift, stagger 0.08 · begins 0.3s after preloader handoff
02Positioning statement

A single short paragraph on what the company does. No animation beyond a line-by-line lift. Deliberate quiet after the hero.

text Lift, stagger 0.08, trigger top 80%
03The journey — scrubbed sequence

The one pinned section on the site. As the user scrolls, the route draws from Port Qasim to the refinery gate, passing four labelled stages: discharge, shore tank, road transit, delivery. A tanker marker travels the drawn path. Stage labels cross-fade as the marker reaches each one.

This is where the concept pays off. Keep it to four stages and roughly two viewport heights of scroll — long enough to feel like a journey, short enough that nobody bails.

path draw Hold, scrub: 1, drawSVG 0 to 100% · marker on motionPath, same scrub
stage labels Snap cross-fade at path progress 0 / 0.33 / 0.66 / 1 · pin duration 200vh · desktop only
04Services

Bulk liquid transport, contract carriage, port clearance, shore tank handling, last-mile delivery. Fill-based blocks rather than floating cards.

blocks Lift, stagger 0.08 · hover Snap, fill shift only — no scale, no shadow
05Contract carriage

The commercial heart of the site. Dedicated fleet, guaranteed capacity, fixed rates. Motion here should feel immovable — a slow reveal, nothing that darts. Consider a single slow tank-fill graphic representing committed capacity.

everything Carry, no stagger — this section arrives as one solid thing
fill graphic Hold, scrubbed, reaching full at section centre
06Operating numbers

Tonnage moved per month, fleet size, on-time percentage, years operating. Tabular figures, aligned.

count-up Hold, scrubbed to scroll position · labels Lift
numbers land on their final value before the section leaves the viewport — never mid-count
07Clients and closing call

Client marks in a continuous marquee — this is the one correct use of linear easing on the entire site, because a loop that eases reveals its seam. Closing block returns to the corridor map, simplified, as a visual bookend.

marquee ease: "none", continuous, pauses on hover · closing map Carry

Network page carries real commercial weight. A prospect checking whether the client serves their lane is a qualified lead. Make lane data genuinely useful — origin, destination, typical transit time, tanker type — rather than decorative. This page will likely outperform the homepage for conversion.

The corridor map is the largest risk to all of these. Simplify the SVG paths aggressively before shipping — a traced highway network can easily carry tens of thousands of path points, and most of that detail is invisible at render size.

Handoff

Before build starts

  • Corridor confirmed with the client — which lanes they actually run, and relative volume by lane
  • City node list finalised, with any depot or shore tank locations they want shown
  • Real operating numbers supplied for the stats section, or the section is cut
  • motion.js committed with the presets above before any animation is written
  • Reduced-motion branch built into the first animation, not retrofitted later
  • Motion reviewed on a real mid-range Android over mobile data, not a desktop throttle profile

Open with the client before design lock. Whether the map should show competitor-visible detail about lanes and volumes. Some carriers treat their route mix as commercially sensitive. Ask before building the concept around it.
